Systematic Literature Review Method

This review should treat each source as evidence to be coded, not merely as a citation to be collected. The goal is to determine what the source can defensibly support, what it cannot support, and how it informs a municipal decision framework for pedestrian heat-mitigation infrastructure.

  1. Log the source. Record citation, URL or DOI, document type, publication year, jurisdiction, publisher, and access date.
  2. Classify the source. Code it as standard, law/guidance, peer-reviewed article, government report, case study, dataset, technical manual, design guide, public-facing article, or internal note.
  3. Extract the research object. Identify the intervention, population, site type, climate context, time scale, and decision context.
  4. Extract methods and metrics. Record whether the source uses field measurement, modeling, survey, GIS, LCCA, MCDA, laboratory testing, policy analysis, design precedent review, or expert judgment.
  5. Separate findings from claims. Distinguish measured results from author interpretation, design intent, advocacy language, or untested assumptions.
  6. Code relevance to SMS. Mark whether the source informs thermal performance, signal/pole constraints, ADA/PROWAG, wind/structural feasibility, lifecycle cost, public acceptance, equity, pedestrian behavior, or implementation governance.
  7. Identify limitations. Note sample size, climate specificity, lack of pedestrian metrics, lack of before/after data, lack of uncertainty analysis, or missing cost/O&M evidence.
  8. Write the synthesis note. Produce a 150-300 word paragraph answering: What does this source add to the decision framework, and what evidence gap remains?
  9. Update the decision matrix. Add one row to the source matrix and one row to the intervention/criterion matrix if the source changes feasibility, scoring, or uncertainty assumptions.

MUTCD

Status: source to be coded. Initial use: hard feasibility gate for signal visibility, traffic-control-device interpretation, signs, markings, and intersection operations.

SMS relevance: Any pole-mounted or intersection-adjacent shade concept must avoid obstruction, confusion, or interference with traffic control.

PROWAG / ADA Public Right-of-Way Accessibility

Status: source to be coded. Initial use: hard feasibility gate for accessible pedestrian routes, curb ramps, clear width, protruding objects, detectable warnings, and pedestrian circulation.

SMS relevance: Shade infrastructure cannot solve heat exposure by creating accessibility barriers.

ASCE 7 Wind and Environmental Loading

Status: source to be coded. Initial use: structural feasibility screen for wind loading, attachments, vibration, uplift, fatigue, and failure risk.

SMS relevance: A shade panel attached to a signal pole creates loads that must be reviewed by qualified structural and traffic-signal engineers.

NACTO Urban Street Design Guidance

Status: source to be coded. Initial use: street-design context, pedestrian realm criteria, transit-street design, and urban public-space framing.

SMS relevance: Useful for design language and pedestrian-priority framing, but not a substitute for local standards or engineering approval.

Urban Heat, Shade, MRT, UTCI, PET, and WBGT Literature

Status: source family to be expanded. Initial use: determine which pedestrian thermal metrics are appropriate for shaded waiting zones at signalized intersections.

SMS relevance: Shade primarily changes radiant load; therefore MRT and derived comfort/stress indices may be more relevant than air temperature alone.

MCDA, LCCA, Robust Decision-Making, and Infrastructure Systems Literature

Status: source family to be expanded. Initial use: build a decision framework that compares shade, trees, cool pavement, shelters, arcades, and hybrid interventions under uncertainty.

SMS relevance: Prevents the project from becoming a single-product advocacy argument. The stronger dissertation contribution is the comparison method.
