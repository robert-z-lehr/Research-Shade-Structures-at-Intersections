▼Research Proposal

Working Title

Decision Analytics for Pedestrian Heat-Mitigation Infrastructure at Urban Intersections.

Current Research Objective

This project is evolving from a single-intervention feasibility study of stoplight-mounted shade structures into a broader decision-support framework for comparing pedestrian heat-mitigation interventions at urban intersections. The central goal is to determine how cities should select, prioritize, reject, pilot, or combine interventions when the decision must account for heat exposure, pedestrian safety, accessibility, lifecycle cost, maintenance, equity, public acceptance, and regulatory constraints.

Core Dissertation-Scale Contribution

The likely PhD-scale contribution is not merely to ask whether SMS works. The stronger contribution is to develop a reproducible decision-analytic methodology for comparing feasible pedestrian heat-mitigation interventions. SMS becomes a motivating case, a candidate intervention, and a test case rather than the only measure of success.

Full Project Scope

The complete research program is intentionally broader than a single pilot installation. It includes a core dissertation scope and several optional or future sub-research tracks. The dissertation should likely center on the decision-support framework, while the additional technical tracks can become supporting analyses, publications, appendices, future grants, or post-dissertation extensions.

1. Intervention Taxonomy

Classify SMS, trees, bus shelters, stand-alone shade, arcades, sidewalk sheds, cool pavement, reflective roofs/walls, misting, and hybrid interventions.

2. Decision Gap Analysis

Build a 30-case matrix identifying where prior interventions lacked full evaluation of cost, thermal performance, equity, operations, regulation, alternatives, or uncertainty.

3. Regulatory Feasibility

Evaluate MUTCD, ADA/PROWAG, ASCE 7 wind loading, AASHTO, right-of-way, signal visibility, utility, and maintenance-access constraints.

4. GIS Site Screening

Prioritize intersections by heat exposure, shade deficit, pedestrian activity, transit access, crash history, vulnerability, sidewalk geometry, and implementation feasibility.

5. Thermal Performance

Compare interventions using pedestrian-relevant metrics such as MRT, UTCI, PET, WBGT, air temperature, surface temperature, and time-weighted exposure.

6. Pedestrian Behavior and Safety

Assess how shade, signal delay, crossing comfort, heat exposure, and intersection geometry may influence waiting, rerouting, transit access, or unsafe crossing behavior.

7. Lifecycle Cost Analysis

Compare capital costs, O&M, replacement cycles, removal, end-of-life, inspection burden, and uncertainty across intervention classes.

8. Pavement and Concrete LCCA

Explore whether shade, albedo, reduced thermal cycling, UV exposure, and water interactions influence asphalt, concrete, sidewalk, or pavement deterioration and lifecycle cost.

9. Vehicle and Fleet Heat Exposure

Optional extension: evaluate whether shaded parking, bus yards, fleet depots, grocery lots, and business-center parking alter vehicle cabin temperatures, degradation, HVAC loads, or maintenance costs.

10. Public Acceptance and Aesthetics

Evaluate whether interventions are perceived as useful, safe, attractive, unserious, obstructive, inequitable, or politically acceptable.

11. MCDA and Uncertainty

Rank alternatives using feasibility gates, weighted criteria, sensitivity analysis, Monte Carlo uncertainty, scenario testing, and Pareto tradeoff analysis.

12. Validation and Transferability

Validate through expert elicitation, precedent back-testing, field measurements, stakeholder review, and comparison across Austin, Dallas, Phoenix, Tempe, LA, NYC, and international precedents.

▼Problem

Many urban intersections expose pedestrians to prolonged solar radiation while waiting to cross streets, access transit, or move through automobile-oriented corridors. In hot environments, this exposure may influence comfort, route choice, transit use, perceived safety, heat-health risk, and the usability of public space.

The planning problem is not only whether one shade device can reduce heat. Cities must decide among many candidate interventions, each with different costs, constraints, benefits, failure modes, and political risks. A tree may provide high-quality shade but require water, time, soil volume, maintenance, and root management. Cool pavement may reduce surface temperature but can alter radiant exposure. Bus shelters may help transit users but not all intersection corners. Sidewalk sheds protect from falling debris but are not designed as climate infrastructure. SMS may be modular and targeted but could face structural, MUTCD, maintenance, and aesthetic constraints.

Therefore, the project frames intersection heat mitigation as an infrastructure decision problem rather than as a single-product feasibility question.

▼Why Shade?

Shade is central because pedestrians experience heat through radiation, air temperature, humidity, wind, surface heat, clothing, metabolic rate, dwell time, and repeated exposure. For waiting pedestrians, shade can directly reduce radiant load even when air temperature changes little.

  • Thermal comfort: shade can reduce MRT, PET, UTCI, and perceived thermal stress.
  • Transportation access: shaded waiting zones may improve walking and transit experience.
  • Equity: pedestrians, transit riders, children, older adults, and outdoor workers are more exposed than people traveling in air-conditioned vehicles.
  • Behavior: uncomfortable waiting conditions may influence crossing choices, route choice, dwell tolerance, or transit avoidance.
  • Asset implications: shade may plausibly affect pavement, concrete, materials, vehicles, and infrastructure exposed to UV radiation and thermal cycling, though those links require separate evidence.
The project should not assume shade is always superior. The purpose is to compare shade, albedo, vegetation, shelters, and hybrid approaches with consistent metrics and constraints.

▼Constraints

Category Example Questions Why It Matters
ADA / PROWAG Does the intervention preserve clear width, curb-ramp access, detectable warnings, and accessible pedestrian routes? A heat intervention that blocks access is not an acceptable public-right-of-way solution.
MUTCD / Signal Visibility Does the intervention obstruct signal heads, signs, sight distance, pedestrian signals, or traffic-control-device interpretation? Traffic-control safety constraints are hard feasibility gates.
Structural / Wind Can poles, brackets, anchors, and shade surfaces withstand wind, vibration, fatigue, and maintenance loads? Especially important for SMS and cantilevered structures.
Operations and Maintenance Who inspects, cleans, removes, replaces, stores, repairs, and funds the intervention? Many pilots fail when O&M ownership is unresolved.
Right-of-Way / Utilities Who owns the pole, sidewalk, signal, curb, underground utilities, and adjacent frontage? Approval authority may be fragmented across city, state, transit, utility, and private actors.
Public Acceptance Is the intervention perceived as useful, safe, dignified, attractive, excessive, obstructive, or unserious? Public interpretation can dominate technical performance.
Lifecycle Cost What are capital, maintenance, replacement, liability, removal, and end-of-life costs over 10, 20, or 30 years? Capital cost alone is insufficient for municipal adoption.

▼Standards & Guidance

The standards module should distinguish hard regulatory constraints from guidance, best practices, and local implementation preferences. It should also identify where a proposed intervention requires interpretation by the authority having jurisdiction.

  • FHWA Manual on Uniform Traffic Control Devices (MUTCD)
  • U.S. Access Board PROWAG and ADA-related public-right-of-way accessibility guidance
  • ASCE 7 wind and environmental loading provisions
  • AASHTO roadway, roadside, pedestrian, and geometric design guidance
  • NACTO Urban Street Design Guide and transit street guidance
  • FHWA pedestrian safety guidance, STEP countermeasures, and intersection safety resources
  • NHTSA/FARS and state crash datasets for fatal and severe crash context
  • Municipal transportation criteria manuals, traffic signal standards, pole attachment rules, and right-of-way manuals
  • Transit agency shelter, bus-stop, and passenger-amenity guidelines

▼Precedents

The precedent review should not only collect examples. It should code each case for what decision analysis was performed, what was omitted, and whether the project was evaluated using metrics relevant to pedestrians rather than only surfaces, aesthetics, or implementation speed.

▼Research Questions

Primary Research Question

How can municipalities systematically compare pedestrian heat-mitigation interventions at urban intersections when each alternative has different thermal benefits, lifecycle costs, maintenance burdens, regulatory constraints, safety implications, equity effects, implementation feasibility, and public-acceptance risks?

Secondary Research Questions

  1. Which prior heat-mitigation or pedestrian-comfort interventions demonstrate incomplete decision analysis, and which evaluation dimensions were omitted?
  2. Which intervention classes are most relevant to intersections: SMS, trees, bus shelters, stand-alone shade, cool pavement, reflective surfaces, sidewalk sheds, arcades, misting, or hybrid packages?
  3. Which constraints should operate as hard feasibility gates before an intervention is scored: MUTCD, ADA/PROWAG, structural wind loading, right-of-way authority, utility conflicts, and maintenance access?
  4. Which thermal metrics are most appropriate for pedestrian waiting conditions: MRT, UTCI, PET, WBGT, surface temperature, air temperature, or time-weighted exposure?
  5. How should GIS be used to identify intersections where heat exposure, pedestrian activity, transit dependence, vulnerability, and feasibility overlap?
  6. How should lifecycle cost analysis include capital cost, O&M, inspection, replacement, removal, failure risk, and uncertainty?
  7. Can shade or reflective interventions plausibly affect pavement, concrete, asphalt, sidewalk, or vehicle lifecycle costs, and what additional evidence would be needed to make those claims defensible?
  8. How might thermal discomfort, long signal delay, lack of shade, and crossing inconvenience influence pedestrian behavior, including waiting, rerouting, transit avoidance, or unsafe crossing?
  9. How should public acceptance, aesthetics, dignity, perceived usefulness, and political feasibility be incorporated into the decision model?
  10. How sensitive are intervention rankings to weights, cost assumptions, site type, climate, maintenance assumptions, and equity priorities?

Candidate Hypotheses

  • H1: Prior heat-mitigation projects frequently omit at least one major decision dimension, such as LCCA, public acceptance, regulatory feasibility, pedestrian-specific thermal metrics, uncertainty, or alternatives comparison.
  • H2: Shade-based interventions will generally outperform surface-reflectance-only interventions on pedestrian radiant heat exposure at waiting locations, though not necessarily on urban surface temperature or implementation cost.
  • H3: SMS will be feasible only for a subset of intersections after hard constraints are applied, but may be competitive where existing poles, shade deficit, pedestrian demand, and right-of-way geometry align.
  • H4: Intervention rankings will change materially when equity weighting, maintenance burden, and lifecycle cost are included instead of capital cost and surface temperature alone.
  • H5: A modular decision-support framework will produce more transparent and transferable recommendations than a single-intervention pilot rationale.

▼GIS + Data Framework

The GIS module identifies candidate intersections and supports spatial prioritization. It should be designed as a defensible screening method, not as a final engineering approval tool.

Layer / Dataset Purpose
Pedestrian counts and transit ridership Estimate exposed users and potential person-hours of benefit.
Tree canopy, building shade, and sky-view factor Measure existing shade and marginal need.
Land surface temperature and heat-index proxies Identify spatial heat-risk patterns while recognizing limits of surface-only metrics.
Sidewalk width, curb ramps, poles, utilities, and ROW Screen feasibility and accessibility constraints.
Crash history: FARS, CRIS, Vision Zero data Provide pedestrian safety context; not proof of heat causation.
Demographics, vulnerability, age, income, car access, disability, and transit dependence Support environmental justice and equity-weighted prioritization.
Signal timing and crossing delay Convert heat intensity into waiting exposure.
Parking lots, bus yards, fleet depots, and high-solar-exposure paved areas Optional extension for pavement, vehicle, and fleet LCCA hypotheses.

▼Pilot Framework

A pilot should occur only after the decision framework identifies a credible intervention-site match. The pilot should be framed as validation of the decision process, not as proof that one intervention is universally superior.

Minimum Pilot Package

  • Site-specific feasibility screen
  • Regulatory and agency review
  • Structural/wind screening for any physical attachment or stand-alone structure
  • ADA/PROWAG review
  • Signal visibility and maintenance-access review
  • Before/after/control field measurement protocol
  • Thermal metrics: MRT, UTCI, PET, WBGT, air temperature, surface temperature, humidity, wind
  • Pedestrian counts, dwell time, and user survey
  • Capital, O&M, inspection, replacement, removal, and end-of-life cost tracking
  • Public acceptance and maintenance feedback

Stakeholder Groups

  • City transportation and public works staff
  • Traffic signal operations and maintenance teams
  • ADA/PROWAG and accessibility reviewers
  • Transit agencies
  • Urban forestry and sustainability offices
  • Public health and heat-resilience groups
  • Neighborhood and business stakeholders
  • Public art and design review groups when aesthetics matter
  • Academic advisors and external technical reviewers
This repository does not claim that candidate concepts are approved, deployable, structurally validated, or operationally feasible without additional engineering review and agency evaluation.
