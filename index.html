Research title

Pedestrian Thermal Exposure at Signalized Intersections

This workspace evaluates whether modular, temporary, permanent, natural, or hybrid shade interventions can reduce pedestrian heat exposure at signalized intersections while remaining safe, accessible, structurally feasible, maintainable, and governable.

Status: preliminary research. This site is not engineering design, construction documentation, legal advice, or agency approval. Claims are labeled as verified, inferred, proposed, draft, or unknown.

Research gap

Verified

Current thermal-routing tools

ASU Cool Routes minimizes distance-weighted mean radiant temperature along walkable network edges. UT Austin CoolPath compares routes using spatially sampled UTCI.

→
Inferred

Possible omission

Published methods do not clearly report corner-specific signal wait time, pedestrian-phase delay, or stationary exposure at actual waiting positions.

Proposed

Intersection-node extension

Represent each signalized corner as a time-dependent exposure node using thermal conditions, arrival time, signal timing, waiting location, and shade geometry.

→
Proposed

Intervention optimization

Ask not only which route is coolest, but where added shade yields the greatest reduction in cumulative person-time exposure.

Total exposure = walking exposure + waiting exposure + crossing exposure

This exploratory calculator uses MRT × minutes as a transparent placeholder. It is not a validated physiological dose model.

Pilot design

Stage 1: citywide screening

Screen 20–50 intersections using pedestrian activity, signal delay, thermal exposure, shade deficit, vulnerability, safety context, transit access, and feasibility.

Stage 2: detailed cases

Select 3–6 intersections for solar geometry, thermal measurements, behavioral observation, structural screening, and standards review.

Stage 3: physical pilot

Install one modular treatment at one or more signalized corners, with before/after and treatment/comparison observations.

Direct and analogous precedents

Direct

Phoenix Sidewalk Shade Project

Artistic shade structures at major intersections and crosswalk waiting areas. Publicly reported behavioral and safety evaluation data remain an open request.

Direct

Los Angeles La Sombrita

Rapid-install shade-and-light prototype. Useful both as precedent and as a warning about insufficient shade geometry and public communication.

Analogous

New York sidewalk sheds

Installed for falling-object and construction protection, not heat. Demonstrate permitting and operation of modular overhead pedestrian structures in constrained rights-of-way.

Conceptual

Temporary floodwalks

Use only when a real installation is verified. The transferable principle is temporary infrastructure deployed during episodic environmental hazards.

Searchable literature notes

Buo et al. (2026), Cool Routes

Verified Uses 1 m hourly MRT rasters and modified Dijkstra routing. Cumulative thermal exposure is reported as distance-weighted MRT along network edges. Publication does not clearly report intersection-specific signal waiting exposure.

Patel et al. (2026), Thermal Comfort Path Planning Tool for Austin

Verified Compares candidate walking and biking routes using UTCI sampled along route geometry. Published description does not clearly report corner-level stationary exposure or signal delay in the thermal objective.

Middel et al., 50 Grades of Shade

Verified Supports treating shade quality as a radiative and geometric continuum rather than a binary condition.

Middel et al., Impact of Shade on Outdoor Thermal Comfort

Verified Direct field evidence for pedestrian-relevant thermal effects of shade in a hot-arid climate.

Middel & Krayenhoff, pedestrian thermal exposure / MaRTy

Verified Methodological basis for mobile pedestrian-scale radiative measurements and validation.

Melnikov et al. (2022), Behavioral Thermal Regulation and Path Choice

Verified Connects solar exposure to route choice and supports estimating tradeoffs between directness and shade.

Roadmap

  1. Verify literature metadata and import complete citation records.
  2. Confirm whether ASU and UT implementations include intersection waiting time outside the published descriptions.
  3. Acquire Phoenix design, thermal, pedestrian-behavior, safety, cost, and maintenance data.
  4. Build Austin intersection screening dataset and document reproducible selection rules.
  5. Conduct standards matrix review with MUTCD, PROWAG, AASHTO, ASCE 7, IBC, and Austin criteria.
  6. Develop signal-delay exposure module and counterfactual shade geometry model.
  7. Prepare pilot protocol, agency consultation package, and ethics / privacy plan for pedestrian observations.

Open questions

  • How much trip exposure is underestimated when signal waiting is omitted?
  • Does including waiting time change the thermally optimal route?
  • Which corner geometry creates the greatest avoidable person-minutes of exposure?
  • How should thermal benefit be normalized across MRT, UTCI, PET, and time?
  • Can temporary canopy systems satisfy visibility, accessibility, wind, drainage, and maintenance constraints?
  • Do shaded waiting areas improve comfort without increasing crowding or obstructing movement?
  • What behavioral and safety data exist for Phoenix’s artistic intersection shade structures?
