▼Research Proposal

Working Title

Decision Analytics for Pedestrian Heat-Mitigation Infrastructure at Urban Intersections.

Current Research Objective

This project is evolving from a single-intervention feasibility study of stoplight-mounted shade structures into a broader decision-support framework for comparing pedestrian heat-mitigation interventions at urban intersections. The central goal is to determine how cities should select, prioritize, reject, pilot, or combine interventions when the decision must account for heat exposure, pedestrian safety, accessibility, lifecycle cost, maintenance, equity, public acceptance, and regulatory constraints.

Core Dissertation-Scale Contribution

The stronger contribution is to develop a reproducible decision-analytic methodology for comparing feasible pedestrian heat-mitigation interventions. SMS becomes a motivating case, candidate intervention, and test case rather than the only measure of success.

1. Intervention Taxonomy

Classify SMS, trees, bus shelters, stand-alone shade, arcades, sidewalk sheds, cool pavement, reflective surfaces, misting, and hybrid interventions.

2. Decision Gap Analysis

Identify where prior interventions omitted cost, thermal performance, equity, operations, regulation, alternatives, or uncertainty.

3. Regulatory Feasibility

Evaluate MUTCD, ADA/PROWAG, ASCE 7 wind loading, AASHTO, right-of-way, signal visibility, utility, and maintenance-access constraints.

4. GIS Site Screening

Prioritize intersections by heat exposure, shade deficit, pedestrian activity, transit access, crash history, vulnerability, sidewalk geometry, and implementation feasibility.

5. Thermal Performance

Compare interventions using MRT, UTCI, PET, WBGT, air temperature, surface temperature, and time-weighted exposure.

6. MCDA + Uncertainty

Rank alternatives using feasibility gates, weighted criteria, sensitivity analysis, Monte Carlo uncertainty, scenario testing, and Pareto tradeoff analysis.

This page is a working research proposal. It does not claim that SMS is approved, structurally validated, legally compliant, cost-effective, or universally suitable. Those are precisely the questions the proposed framework is intended to evaluate.

▼Problem

Many urban intersections expose pedestrians to prolonged solar radiation while waiting to cross streets, access transit, or move through automobile-oriented corridors. The planning problem is not only whether one shade device can reduce heat. Cities must decide among many candidate interventions, each with different costs, constraints, benefits, failure modes, and political risks.

Therefore, the project frames intersection heat mitigation as an infrastructure decision problem rather than as a single-product feasibility question.

▼Why Shade?

Shade is central because pedestrians experience heat through radiation, air temperature, humidity, wind, surface heat, clothing, metabolic rate, dwell time, and repeated exposure. For waiting pedestrians, shade can directly reduce radiant load even when air temperature changes little.

  • Thermal comfort: shade can reduce MRT, PET, UTCI, and perceived thermal stress.
  • Transportation access: shaded waiting zones may improve walking and transit experience.
  • Equity: pedestrians, transit riders, children, older adults, and outdoor workers are more exposed than people traveling in air-conditioned vehicles.
  • Behavior: uncomfortable waiting conditions may influence crossing choices, route choice, dwell tolerance, or transit avoidance.

▼Constraints

CategoryExample QuestionsWhy It Matters
ADA / PROWAGDoes the intervention preserve clear width, curb-ramp access, detectable warnings, and accessible pedestrian routes?A heat intervention that blocks access is not an acceptable public-right-of-way solution.
MUTCD / Signal VisibilityDoes the intervention obstruct signal heads, signs, sight distance, pedestrian signals, or traffic-control-device interpretation?Traffic-control safety constraints are hard feasibility gates.
Structural / WindCan poles, brackets, anchors, and shade surfaces withstand wind, vibration, fatigue, and maintenance loads?Especially important for SMS and cantilevered structures.
O&MWho inspects, cleans, removes, replaces, stores, repairs, and funds the intervention?Many pilots fail when O&M ownership is unresolved.
Lifecycle CostWhat are capital, maintenance, replacement, liability, removal, and end-of-life costs over 10, 20, or 30 years?Capital cost alone is insufficient for municipal adoption.

▼Standards & Guidance

The standards module should distinguish hard regulatory constraints from guidance, best practices, and local implementation preferences. It should also identify where a proposed intervention requires interpretation by the authority having jurisdiction.

  • FHWA Manual on Uniform Traffic Control Devices (MUTCD)
  • U.S. Access Board PROWAG and ADA-related public-right-of-way accessibility guidance
  • ASCE 7 wind and environmental loading provisions
  • AASHTO roadway, roadside, pedestrian, and geometric design guidance
  • NACTO Urban Street Design Guide and transit street guidance
  • Municipal transportation criteria manuals, traffic signal standards, pole attachment rules, and right-of-way manuals

▼Research Questions

Primary Research Question

How can municipalities systematically compare pedestrian heat-mitigation interventions at urban intersections when each alternative has different thermal benefits, lifecycle costs, maintenance burdens, regulatory constraints, safety implications, equity effects, implementation feasibility, and public-acceptance risks?

Candidate Hypotheses

  • Prior heat-mitigation projects frequently omit at least one major decision dimension, such as LCCA, public acceptance, regulatory feasibility, pedestrian-specific thermal metrics, uncertainty, or alternatives comparison.
  • SMS will be feasible only for a subset of intersections after hard constraints are applied, but may be competitive where existing poles, shade deficit, pedestrian demand, and right-of-way geometry align.
  • Intervention rankings will change materially when equity weighting, maintenance burden, and lifecycle cost are included instead of capital cost and surface temperature alone.

▼Pilot Framework

A pilot should occur only after the decision framework identifies a credible intervention-site match. The pilot should be framed as validation of the decision process, not as proof that one intervention is universally superior.

  • Site-specific feasibility screen
  • Regulatory and agency review
  • Structural/wind screening for any physical attachment or stand-alone structure
  • ADA/PROWAG review
  • Signal visibility and maintenance-access review
  • Before/after/control field measurement protocol
  • Thermal metrics: MRT, UTCI, PET, WBGT, air temperature, surface temperature, humidity, wind
  • Pedestrian counts, dwell time, and user survey
  • Capital, O&M, inspection, replacement, removal, and end-of-life cost tracking

Literature Review Workflow

For each source, log the citation; classify the source type; extract the research object; extract methods and metrics; separate findings from claims; code relevance to SMS; identify limitations; write a 150-300 word synthesis note; and update the decision matrix.

Daily / Weekly Repository Method

Complete at least one concrete repository task per day. A task is complete only when it leaves a visible artifact: a coded source row, issue, commit, synthesis note, diagram, table, or revised section. On Sunday, write a weekly reflection covering completed work, blockers, evidence found, evidence gaps, and the next Monday-Sunday plan.

▼Terminology

TermWorking DefinitionWhy It Matters
MRTMean radiant temperature: a summary measure of radiant heat exchange between the body and surrounding surfaces, sky, sun, and shade.Often more relevant than air temperature for pedestrian shade because shade primarily reduces radiant load.
WBGTWet Bulb Globe Temperature: a heat-stress index combining air temperature, humidity, radiant heat, and wind effects.Useful for occupational heat stress and public-health framing.
UTCIUniversal Thermal Climate Index: an outdoor thermal-stress index based on air temperature, humidity, wind, and radiation.Useful for comparing outdoor thermal stress across places and scenarios.
PETPhysiological Equivalent Temperature: a thermal comfort index expressing outdoor conditions as an equivalent indoor air temperature.Common in urban climate and pedestrian comfort studies.
LCCALifecycle Cost Analysis: a method for comparing costs over an asset's useful life.Prevents overvaluing low capital cost while ignoring long-term municipal burden.
MCDAMulti-Criteria Decision Analysis: a family of methods for ranking alternatives across multiple objectives.Core framework for comparing shade, trees, cool pavement, shelters, and hybrid interventions.
